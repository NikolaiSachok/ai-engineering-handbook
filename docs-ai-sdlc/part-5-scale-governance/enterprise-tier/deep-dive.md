# The machinery of proof

[Part 1](./index.md) argued that at the enterprise tier a control counts only when someone other than its
operator can demonstrate afterwards that it ran — proof, not just capability. It named audit,
non-repudiation, provenance, and separation of duties, and left the mechanics as "signed commits, an SBOM,
signed attestations." This page names the actual frameworks, because at this tier the specifics are the
lesson: a ladder of provenance strength (SLSA), the two inventory standards (SPDX and CycloneDX), the
signing layer that makes any of it non-repudiable (in-toto and Sigstore), and the agent-shaped question
none of them was designed for. The honest limit comes last, because it is the part that gets oversold.

## SLSA: provenance as a ladder, not a checkbox

Provenance is not on-or-off; it comes in strengths, and [SLSA](https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.0/levels)'s build
track puts numbers on them. Read the levels as answers to "how hard is this provenance to forge"
(`ASSERTED`, per the specification):

- **Build L1** — the artifact carries provenance describing how it was built. It may be unsigned. It catches
  honest mistakes and is trivial to forge, which is exactly its stated purpose: a floor, not a defence.
- **Build L2** — the provenance is **signed by a hosted build platform**, so forging it requires an actual
  attack rather than a configuration mistake. The build runs somewhere that signs on your behalf.
- **Build L3** — the platform is **tamper-resistant, builds are isolated from each other, and the signing key
  is inaccessible to user-defined build steps.** That last clause is the load-bearing one for a fleet: the
  thing that *runs the build* cannot reach the key that *vouches for it*.

That ladder is the introduction's blast-radius rule made into infrastructure: each rung buys a stronger proof
at a real cost, and you climb it only as far as the distance from the blast radius justifies. For agent work
L3's separation has a sharp reading — **the agent that generated the code must not hold the key that attests
to it**, the same separation-of-duties argument from
[Part 1](./index.md), now enforced by the platform instead of asserted in a policy.

## SBOM: the inventory, in one of two dialects

A **software bill of materials** is the list of what went into an artifact — every dependency, version, and
license — so that a newly-disclosed vulnerability becomes a lookup instead of an investigation. Two standards
dominate, and they come from different worlds (`ASSERTED`):

- **[SPDX](https://spdx.dev/)** — a Linux Foundation standard (and ISO/IEC 5962), a package-and-relationship
  model, historically compliance- and license-focused.
- **[CycloneDX](https://cyclonedx.org/)** — an OWASP standard, a compact component-centric model, security-
  and vulnerability-focused, and it has grown to cover services, ML models, and cryptography.

The choice between them is real but secondary; the discipline is that the SBOM exists, is generated by the
build rather than written by hand, and is kept current. Agent-generated code sharpens the need: a fleet pulls
in dependencies at machine speed, so the inventory a human might have tracked informally now has to be
produced mechanically or it is simply wrong.

## in-toto and Sigstore: what makes it non-repudiable

An SBOM and a provenance statement are just documents until something binds them to the build and makes them
un-editable after the fact — which is Part 1's **non-repudiation** requirement, now concrete. Two pieces carry
it:

- **[in-toto attestations](https://in-toto.io/)** are the envelope: a signed statement of the form *this
  subject (the artifact, by hash) has this predicate (its provenance, its SBOM)*. SLSA provenance is itself an
  in-toto predicate, and an SBOM in either dialect can be wrapped the same way — the envelope is format-
  agnostic.
- **[Sigstore](https://www.sigstore.dev/)** (via `cosign`) is the signing layer, and its useful trick is
  **keyless signing**: instead of a long-lived key someone must guard, it issues a short-lived certificate
  bound to a workload identity through OIDC, and logs the signature in a public transparency log. That short-
  lived, identity-bound credential is the [least-privilege](../../part-4-platform/least-privilege-sandboxing/index.md) rule from
  Part IV applied to signing itself.

Stacked, they answer three different questions that are easy to conflate: the SBOM says *what is inside*, the
provenance says *how and by what it was built*, and the signature says *this cannot be repudiated or altered*.
A record an operator could edit afterwards is a story; a signature the operator's own build step could not
produce is evidence.

## Separation of duties, when both parties are agents

Part 1 made separation of duties an engineering argument and then a compliance one. The machinery is where the
two meet. SLSA L3's "signing key inaccessible to build steps" and the classic **two-party review** — one actor
produces, a different one approves — are the same principle at different layers, and both survive the move to a
fleet only if the actors are **genuinely distinct identities with distinct credentials**, and the record shows
which was which. The generating agent runs under one identity; the reviewing agent under another; the human
sign-off is a real signature by a real person.

That last point is where the tier's own failure mode lives, and it is worth stating flatly because the
signature is now legally meaningful. A human approving changes at a rate no human could actually read is not
oversight — it is the [review-at-volume](../../part-3-verification/review-at-volume.md) failure wearing a
compliance signature. The machinery makes the approval *provable*; it cannot make it *true*. That is the job
of the layers below it.

## The honest limit: provenance is not correctness

The thing this machinery proves is narrow, and overselling it is the standard mistake. A fully attested,
SLSA-L3, SBOM-carrying, Sigstore-signed artifact can still be **wrong** — insecure, buggy, subtly incorrect.
Provenance answers "did this artifact come from that source through that process, unaltered." It does not
answer "is the artifact any good." That question belongs to the entire rest of the course: the
[gate chain](../../part-3-verification/layered-gates/index.md), the tests, the review. Supply-chain integrity
and software correctness are orthogonal, and a program that has invested heavily in the first can still ship a
confidently-attested defect.

So the tier earns its cost by its position, not by superiority. It sits farthest from the blast radius, where
the job is *proof*, and it buys proof at the price of real infrastructure — a build platform you trust, a
signing service, a transparency log, an inventory pipeline. You climb the ladder as far as the stakes justify
and no further, and you never let the strength of the provenance be mistaken for the quality of the thing whose
provenance it is.

## What to take away

- **SLSA is a ladder, not a checkbox:** L1 provenance exists (forgeable), L2 is signed by a hosted platform
  (forging needs an attack), L3 makes the signing key **inaccessible to the build steps** — which, for a fleet,
  means the agent that built the artifact cannot sign for it.
- **An SBOM is the inventory** in one of two dialects — SPDX (Linux Foundation, compliance/licence) or
  CycloneDX (OWASP, security/vulnerability). It must be **generated by the build**, because a fleet pulls
  dependencies faster than anyone tracks them by hand.
- **in-toto + Sigstore make it non-repudiable:** in-toto is the signed envelope binding a predicate to an
  artifact by hash; Sigstore's keyless signing binds the signature to a short-lived workload identity — least
  privilege applied to signing.
- **The three answers don't collapse into one:** SBOM = *what's inside*, provenance = *how it was built*,
  signature = *cannot be altered or repudiated*.
- **Separation of duties has to be distinct identities,** enforced by the platform, with a human sign-off that
  a human could actually perform — otherwise it is the review-at-volume failure with a legal signature.
- **Provenance is not correctness.** All of this proves an artifact's origin and integrity, never its quality;
  a fully attested artifact can still be wrong, which is why the rest of the course exists.
